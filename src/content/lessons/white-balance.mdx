## Light has a color

Your eyes adjust so that white paper looks white under a lamp, in the shade, or at noon. A camera has to be told. That's **<Term id="white-balance">white balance</Term>**.

Light color is measured in **<Term id="kelvin">kelvin (K)</Term>**:

| Light | Approx. K | Looks |
|---|---|---|
| Candle | 1900K | Deep orange |
| Household bulb (tungsten) | 2700–3200K | Warm orange |
| Fluorescent / LED | 4000–5000K | Neutral to greenish |
| Midday sun | 5200–5600K | Neutral |
| Overcast | 6500K | Slightly blue |
| Open shade | 7000–8000K | Blue |

The setting tells the camera *what the light is*, so it can cancel it. Set 3000K under warm bulbs and the camera adds blue to neutralize them.

## Auto, presets, or kelvin?

- **Auto WB (AWB)** is good in most daylight. It can struggle under warm indoor light or at sunset, where it may "correct away" the mood.
- **Presets** (Daylight, Cloudy, Shade, Tungsten) are fine and consistent.
- **Kelvin** gives precise control, and every frame of a series matches.
- **Custom WB** with a grey card gives exact color for product shots.

## Shoot RAW if you can

A **<Term id="raw">RAW</Term>** file stores white balance as a *note*, not baked into the pixels, so you can change it later with no quality loss. In JPEG, white balance is permanent. RAW also keeps 1–2 more stops of highlight and shadow detail. The cost is bigger files that need editing.
