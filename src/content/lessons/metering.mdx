## How the meter thinks

Your camera's light meter measures the scene and suggests an exposure. The catch: **it assumes the world averages out to <Term id="middle-grey">middle grey</Term>.** For most scenes that's a good guess. But:

- **Bright scenes** (snow, beaches, white walls) get *underexposed*. The meter darkens them toward grey.
- **Dark scenes** (black cat, night club, dark suit) get *overexposed*. The meter brightens them toward grey.
- **Backlit subjects** (person in front of a window) turn into silhouettes, because the bright background dominates.

### Metering modes

- **Evaluative / Matrix**: looks at the whole frame, smartly weighted. The default.
- **Center-weighted**: favors the middle of the frame.
- **Spot**: measures a tiny area. Point it at something mid-toned (grass, skin, grey jeans) for a precise reading.

## Exposure compensation

In Av, Tv, P, or M with Auto ISO, **<Term id="exposure-comp">exposure compensation</Term>** tells the camera "brighter than you think" (+) or "darker" (−).

- Snow, sand, bright sky: **+1 to +2**
- Dark subjects, night scenes: **−⅔ to −1**
- Backlit portrait (no flash): **+1 to +2** (or spot meter the face)

## Reading the histogram

The **<Term id="histogram">histogram</Term>** is a graph of every pixel's brightness: blacks on the left, whites on the right. It's more reliable than the screen on the back of your camera, which can look bright in a dark room and dim in the sun.

- **Bunched left**: dark / underexposed.
- **Bunched right**: bright / overexposed.
- **Piled against an edge**: **<Term id="clipping">clipping</Term>**, meaning detail lost to pure black or pure white.

There's no "correct" shape. A night scene *should* sit to the left. What matters is not clipping things you care about.

The **highlight warning** (the blinking stripes on overexposed areas, often called "blinkies") shows you exactly what's clipped. Turn it on in your camera's playback menu.

### ETTR

**<Term id="ettr">Expose to the right</Term>**: when shooting RAW, make the exposure as bright as possible without clipping highlights, then darken it in editing. Brighter exposures have cleaner shadows. Just don't blow the highlights: once they hit pure white, the detail can't be recovered.
