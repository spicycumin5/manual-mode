## What a photo actually is

A camera is a dark box that collects light for a moment. **Exposure** is how much light it collects, and that decides how bright the picture looks. Too little and the photo is dark and murky. Too much and the bright parts turn into blank white.

In manual mode you control exposure with three settings:

- **Aperture**: how *wide* the lens opening is.
- **Shutter speed**: how *long* the opening stays open.
- **ISO**: how much the camera *amplifies* what it collected.

Think of filling a bucket from a tap. Aperture is how far you open the tap, shutter speed is how long you leave it running, and ISO is like having a smaller bucket that looks full sooner.

## Stops: the unit of light

Photographers measure light in **<Term id="stop">stops</Term>**. One stop is a doubling (or halving) of light. That is the whole trick: every setting is marked in stops, so you can trade one for another.

<StopsDemo />

- Shutter 1/125 → 1/60 s: open twice as long, **+1 stop**.
- ISO 200 → 400: twice the amplification, **+1 stop**.
- Aperture f/8 → f/5.6: twice the area, **+1 stop**.

Cameras let you move in ⅓-stop clicks, so three clicks on any dial = one stop.

## The exposure triangle

Each setting changes brightness, **and each has a side effect.** That side effect is the creative part of photography:

| Setting | Brighter when you… | Side effect |
|---|---|---|
| Aperture | Open it (smaller f-number) | Shallower **depth of field**: blurrier background |
| Shutter speed | Slow it down | More **motion blur** and risk of camera shake |
| ISO | Raise it | More **noise** (grain) |

Because they all work in stops, you can swap them. Open the aperture one stop and speed the shutter up one stop: the brightness is identical, but the picture looks different. These are called **equivalent exposures**.

## How bright is the world?

Light levels differ enormously: a sunny beach is about 20 stops brighter than a starry night. That is why the same settings can't work everywhere.

<LightLevels />

<Mistakes>
- Thinking ISO "lets in" more light. It doesn't. It amplifies the light you already captured, including the noise.
- Forgetting that the f-number runs backwards: **f/2 is wide open, f/16 is a pinhole**.
- Chasing a "perfect" meter reading. The meter is advice; you'll learn when to overrule it.
</Mistakes>
